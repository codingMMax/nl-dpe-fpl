Why an attention head wants the small crossbar

One head of attention costs least on the smallest array of the DSE, 128 × 64: the least area, the least energy and the lowest cycles × area of any array (only ViT-B/16's 128 × 128 has fewer cycles, by 9 %, at 30 % more area). This page shows the cost of each stage, how it changes with the array, and why no mapping choice reverses it. The numbers are the cycle-accurate simulator's and VTR's, for the smallest and the largest head of the DSE: JetFormer-150P (n 32, dmodel 64, d 32), ViT-B/16 (n 256, dmodel 768, d 64).

1. The head in five stages

Every stage works in passes of one crossbar. The work of a stage is fixed by the head; the mapping decides how many crossbars share it.

The projections' crossbar count is set by capacity: a bigger tile holds more of the matrix, so fewer tiles are needed. Every other stage's count is set by bandwidth: the number of ports needed to keep pace with the score stream. A bigger crossbar has the same 40-bit port, so those counts do not change with the array.

The time diagrams: JetFormer-150P

  • The stream. The projections push all 32 tokens through their ports, one token every 21 cycles. The other stages work on blocks of 16 keys. The light bars are the LOG conversion of each token's rows into the log domain as it arrives, one short pass per row; they never fall behind.
  • The wait. A score block needs all S queries of its run. Here S = n = 32, one run, so the first score block needs the last token and starts at cycle 711, when the stream ends at 683. The keys were ready long before.
  • The chain. Each block then passes through scores, softmax, PV and rescale in turn, each stage starting when the one before delivers. With 2 blocks the pipeline never fills: the 596 cycles after the stream are one pipeline latency plus the run's tails, the log l read-out and the normalization. Doubling any stage's crossbars would not shorten them.
  • Overlaps. Where a block starts before the previous one ends, the later block is drawn lighter: the softmax reads the next tile while it finishes the previous block, and the rescale runs two blocks on two crossbar groups.
  • From 128 × 64 to 512 × 256. A token takes 53 cycles instead of 21: the same tiles, but every pass now drains all the wider array's columns. The stream ends at 1,675 instead of 683, and the first score block starts at 1,703 instead of 711. The head takes 2,579 cycles instead of 1,279, 2.0×. The time after the stream is 904 cycles against 596: the core's passes are longer on the wider array, but the core does not get faster.

The time diagrams: ViT-B/16

  • The first run overlaps the stream. S = 64 < n = 256, so the first run needs only the first 64 tokens and starts at cycle 2,230, while the stream runs to 8,724. Its blocks come at the stream's pace, a 16-key block every 16 × 34 = 544 cycles, so every stage works in bursts and waits for keys.
  • The later runs come at the core's pace. After the stream, a block every 309 cycles: the hand-over between the softmax and PV holds two blocks, so block k + 2 cannot start before PV has consumed block k. Blocks come in pairs, and every stage idles between pairs.
  • Every run ends with its tails, the log l read-out and the normalization: the small dark boxes.
  • From 128 × 64 to 512 × 256. A token takes 111 cycles instead of 34: fewer tiles hold the matrices, so fewer ports feed the stream. The stream ends at 28,434 instead of 8,724, and the first score block starts at 28,468 instead of 2,230. The head takes 45,252 cycles instead of 24,395, +85 %. With S = 256 instead of 64 there is 1 run instead of 4, and the time after the stream is 16,818 cycles against 15,671.

3. What one crossbar delivers, and what it costs

A pass loads I = min(R, C) rows of 8-bit values through the 40-bit port, 5 bytes per cycle, then runs the 8 bit-slices, one bit plane of the input per cycle. The C output columns drain at ⌈8C / 40⌉ + 1 cycles; the drain overlaps the next load and binds only when C is wider than the rows loaded, which happens to the projections of a small head, not to these passes. A pass converts I values, one per row loaded, because the stored weights are identity blocks. So the pass period is τ(I) = ⌈8I / 40⌉ + 8, and the time per value is 1/5 + 8/I cycles: the 1/5 is the port and never changes, the 8/I is the pipeline spread over the rows of one pass.

The two constants are the hard block's, not the mapping's: a 40-bit port and 8 bit-slices in every array. The simulator's pass period is gated against the RTL on the 64 × 64 cases. A pass fires all R × C cells whatever it uses, and a crossbar's area is its R × C tile plus soft logic sized by its window I.

From 128 × 64 to 512 × 256 a crossbar fires 16× the cells and converts 1.4× the values per cycle. Bandwidth is bought per crossbar, and a crossbar costs R × C cells. So a unit of bandwidth costs about 11× more cells on the large array.

  • Cycles. The projections account for most of the extra cycles (JetFormer-150P 992 of 1,300; ViT-B/16 19,710 of 20,857). A big tile holds more of the matrix, so there are fewer tiles and fewer ports for the token stream; where the tiles do not change, the wider drain does it. The online softmax reader paces everything else at 5 scores per cycle on any array, so tiled QK and tiled PV do not get faster even though each of their crossbars does.
  • Energy. Tiled QK and tiled PV fire 16× the cells per pass for 1.4× the values per cycle. Their energy rises (JetFormer-150P +34 %, 80 % of the head's energy; ViT-B/16 +16 %, 91 % of the head's energy). The projections are a few percent of the energy at any array.
  • Area. The bandwidth-set stages keep their crossbars and the tiles grow 16×. The soft logic and the memory blocks are most of the area (JetFormer-150P 68 %; ViT-B/16 70 % at 512 × 256), and the soft logic grows with the window I.
  • So every stage's cost goes up. The only gain, 1.4× per crossbar in tiled QK and tiled PV, is one no stage can use.

6. Why sweeping the mapping does not change the answer

Every mapping knob adds crossbars to a stage: Pproj copies the projection tiles, NE, NEP, NEXP and NOC widen the other stages. A crossbar brings one port and costs one R × C tile, so the cycles × area of a stage can only get worse with the array at any knob setting. The large array can buy back the projections' speed with copies, but it pays 16× more cells per copy than the small one.

We checked this with the per-build optimum over the knobs (mapping_sensitivity.py --optimize). Giving every build its own best Pproj, NE, NEP, NEXP and Bkv cuts its ADP to 0.25 to 0.79 of the fixed mapping (JetFormer-150P 0.42 to 0.58; ViT-B/16 0.33 to 0.79), and across the six heads only positions 4 and 5 swap (128 × 128 and 1024 × 64). 128 × 64 stays first. (This check ran on 12 configs with the crossbar tiles of that day; it is not yet re-run on the 8 configs and the row-parallel tiles of 2026-10-22.)

Two choices in the design are not knobs: a run holds S = min(n, I) queries, and every pass drains all C columns. Both cost the large arrays something: by the equations (worked on the 12 arrays of 2026-10-19), about 10 % of the cycles at ViT-B/16 and up to 40 % at JetFormer-150P on the 256-column arrays. Removing both would still leave the product of cycles and area at more than twice that of 128 × 64.

7. Why the same hardware likes big arrays for FC

An FC layer's weight matrix is large: 2048 × 256 is 524,288 cells, which is 64 tiles of 128 × 64 or 4 tiles of 512 × 256. Its crossbar count is set by capacity, so a bigger array means fewer crossbars and less soft logic around them. The area falls from 0.88 to 0.33 mm² while the cycles rise from 54 to 168. That is a real trade-off, and the FC front spans several arrays. Attention's matrices are small (dmodel × d, and identity blocks of d × d), and its counts are set by bandwidth, so a bigger array only adds cells.

Attention needs ports, not cells. The small array has the most ports per cell, so it wins by structure, not by tuning.
